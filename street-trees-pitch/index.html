Pitch to The Pudding

Who gets the healthy street trees?

This is a pitch for a visual essay built on New York City's street tree census. It tests one question against the decision path The Pudding uses, and it lists what is still open. Nothing here is a result yet.

01 · The pitch

The one-minute version

New York's street trees were counted block by block in 2015, with each tree's species, size, and health recorded. Some neighborhoods have dense, shady streets. Others have a few sickly trees and a lot of bare pavement. Do the places with the fewest trees also have the trees in the worst shape? If so, the difference shows up on every block, and we can show it one tree at a time.

The essay would map each tree as a dot, then zoom into a few blocks to show what "Poor" health looks like on the sidewalk.

03 · The plan

How the data would be used

The census lists trees, so the first job is to combine it with census tracts. Each step below states what gets done and what could go wrong.

StepWhat happensWhat could go wrong
1. Get the tree recordsDownload the 2015 Street Tree Census tree records from the NYC Open Data portal.The mirrors we checked do not state a license. We need the original listing and its terms first.
2. Get neighborhood numbersJoin each tree to its census tract, then pull population and median household income for each tract from the American Community Survey.Tract boundaries and survey years differ from the 2015 count, so the numbers need matching carefully.
3. Measure tree densityCount trees per 1,000 residents in each tract, and count trees per tract area as a second measure.Tracts with few residents can give extreme ratios. We would set a minimum size.
4. Measure conditionCalculate the share of trees rated Poor and the share rated Good in each tract.Health ratings are volunteer judgments made in one visit, so two volunteers could rate the same tree differently.
5. CompareTest whether low-density tracts have a higher share of Poor trees, and whether income explains the pattern.Both density and income can move together. The comparison needs to hold one factor fixed while looking at the other.
6. Check the storyVisit a few blocks in each group with an urban forester, and ask the local parks office whether the numbers match what they see.If the blocks disagree with the numbers, the story changes or gets dropped.
04 · Open questions

What still needs an answer

  • License. Confirm the terms on the original NYC Open Data listing before any data appears in the essay.
  • Age of the data. The census is from 2015. Check whether a newer street tree count exists, and whether a newer count would change the question.
  • Who should tell this story. The pitch has no arborist or local urban forestry voice yet. The Pudding asks this question at a late gate, so it needs a real answer.
  • Consent and framing. The essay should not suggest that residents in low-tree areas are to blame for their trees' health.
