Street trees · New York City

One bug away

New York's sidewalks hold more than 650,000 trees. In most neighborhoods, a few kinds of tree do most of the work, and the insects that kill trees are picky about which kinds they attack.

Orange is maple, teal is London plane, green is oak, yellow is honey locust, white is Callery pear, and blue is ash.

A few kinds do most of the work

Sorted by kind, the same trees show how lopsided the city's planting is. Maples, London planes, and oaks each make up more than a tenth of all street trees.

Foresters often cite a rule of thumb credited to Frank Santamour: no single species should be more than 10 percent of a city's trees, no genus more than 20 percent, and no family more than 30 percent. London plane is a single species, and it makes up of the city's street trees on its own.

The beetle's favorite trees

The Asian longhorned beetle lays its eggs in living trees, and its larvae tunnel through the wood. It prefers maples, elms, horse chestnuts, willows, and birches. Those trees, shown in red, make up this share of the city's street trees. They are concentrated in eastern Queens and Staten Island.

How an outbreak could travel

The beetle was first found in the United States in Greenpoint, Brooklyn, in 1996. This animation starts with the host trees nearest Greenpoint and lets an infestation jump from host tree to host tree. At each step, an infected tree can reach host trees within about a kilometer.

This is an illustration with simple, made-up rules. Real outbreaks move more slowly, and crews remove infested trees to stop them. The beetle has since been cleared from parts of the city.
