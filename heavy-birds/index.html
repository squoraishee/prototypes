01 Wildlife strikes on U.S. aircraft, 2025

Heavy Birds

Pilots, airports and airlines reported 24,457 collisions between aircraft and wildlife in the United States in 2025, the most since the FAA's records begin in 1990. That is about 67 a day. Most were small birds that did no harm. The damage came from a few heavy species. Through August, 2026 is running 4% ahead of last year.

02 Reported strikes, 1990 to 2025

Eleven times as many reports as in 1990

The FAA logged 2,120 strikes in 1990 and 24,457 in 2025. Damaging strikes rose more slowly, from 371 to 905, so the share of strikes that damaged the aircraft fell from 17.5% to 3.7%. That pattern suggests much of the rise is better reporting of minor strikes that once went unrecorded.

03 2025 strikes by size of the animal

Small birds make up the count. Large ones do the damage.

Where a report gives the animal's size, large birds and mammals were 5% of 2025's strikes but 35% of the strikes that damaged an aircraft. A small bird damaged the aircraft about 1 time in 60. A large animal damaged it about 1 time in 4.

04 213 species and groups, 2015 to 2025

Which animals damage aircraft

Mourning doves were the most-struck species from 2015 to 2025, with 10,955 strikes, but only 1.4% caused damage. Three in four strikes on a white-tailed deer damaged the aircraft. Among birds, black vultures did the most harm per strike, at 64%. Turkey vultures ran up $50 million in reported repairs, the most of any bird.

05 Strikes in 2020 to 2024 against 1990 to 1994

Eagles, ospreys and vultures are being hit more often

Reported strikes of all kinds grew 6.9 times between the early 1990s and the early 2020s. Bald eagle strikes grew 13 times, osprey strikes 11 times and black vulture strikes from 2 to 141. All three populations have grown. In 2021 the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service estimated the lower 48 states had about 316,700 bald eagles, roughly four times its 2009 estimate. Canada goose strikes grew far less than the total, and deer strikes fell.

06 Black vulture strikes by state

Black vulture strikes have spread north

From 1990 to 2004, black vulture strikes were reported in 4 states and Washington, D.C., most of them in Florida. From 2015 to 2025 they were reported in 21 states and D.C., as far north as New York, Pennsylvania and Illinois. The North American Breeding Bird Survey finds black vultures increased about 3.4% a year from 1966 to 2019, as the species spread north.

Darker states had more black vulture strikes in the period, and more vultures circle them. The map plays through the three periods once, then the buttons pick one. A strike's state is where the airport is, and strike counts also depend on how many airports in a state report.

07 Height above the ground, 2015 to 2025

Most strikes happen near the ground. Higher ones do more harm.

Of the strikes from 2015 to 2025 with a reported height, 45% happened on the runway, and 77% at or below 1,000 feet. Higher up, aircraft fly faster and a strike carries more energy. On the runway, 3.8% of strikes damaged the aircraft. Above 10,000 feet, where migrating birds sometimes fly, 21% did.

08 Method

How the numbers were built

Data

Every strike comes from the FAA Wildlife Strike Database, downloaded November 8, 2026. It holds 359,793 reports of civil aircraft hitting birds and other wildlife in the United States, and U.S. aircraft abroad, from 1990 to October 28, 2026. Pilots, airports, airlines and engine makers file the reports. The FAA and the U.S. Department of Agriculture check them, and the Smithsonian identifies species from remains.

A damaging strike is one rated minor, uncertain or substantial damage, or destroyed. Size is the reporter's estimate. Growth on page 5 compares 1990 to 1994 against 2020 to 2024, five years each, to smooth out small counts.

Airports are placed with coordinates from OurAirports, matched on the FAA's airport code.

Limits

  • Reporting is voluntary for most operators. The FAA has estimated that many strikes go unreported, so counts show what was reported, not every strike.
  • Reporting has grown faster than strikes. Comparisons between species, or against all strikes, are fairer than raw counts over time.
  • Many reports don't identify the species, the size, the height or the damage. Each figure uses the reports that do, and says how many.
  • Reports for recent months are still coming in. 2026 is compared with 2025 through August only.
