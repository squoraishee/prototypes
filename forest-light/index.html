Forest light

Reading the light under a forest

Stand under a tree in summer and the light looks green, even though sunlight is nearly white. The leaves above you have taken the light apart, kept some wavelengths, and sent the rest somewhere else. What reaches the ground is a different light again.

Spectroscopy is the tool for reading that change. It measures how much light there is at each wavelength, not just how bright it is. The same measurement, made from a satellite, tells scientists how healthy a forest is, how much water it holds, and how much carbon it is storing.

The figures below are models built from the shapes of real leaf and sunlight spectra. They show the physics clearly. They are not measurements of any particular tree.

01

Sunlight is not one color

A hot object glows at a range of wavelengths, and the sun's spectrum peaks in the green-blue part of the visible range.

The sun's surface is about 5,800 kelvin. A hot body emits light at every wavelength, and the strength at each wavelength follows Planck's law. Most of the sun's energy falls between about 400 and 1000 nanometers, the range that matters for plants.

The shaded bands mark the visible range (400–700 nm) and the near infrared (700–1000 nm). Plants use the visible range for photosynthesis. Their structure responds strongly to the near infrared.

02

A leaf is a colored filter

Chlorophyll absorbs red and blue light strongly, reflects green, and leaves the near infrared mostly alone.

Each leaf reflects some wavelengths, transmits some, and absorbs the rest. The absorbed light drives photosynthesis. The sharp rise near 700 nanometers, called the red edge, is one of the clearest features in any plant spectrum. It appears because chlorophyll stops absorbing there while the leaf's internal structure keeps scattering light back.

03

Light sinks through the canopy

Each layer of leaves removes more light from the blue and red than from the far red, so the light that reaches the ground is shifted.

Canopy depth is measured by the leaf area index, or LAI: the total leaf area above a square meter of ground. A grassland has an LAI near 1. A dense tropical forest can reach 5 or 6. The model below attenuates each wavelength with an exponential law, the Beer-Lambert law, using the leaf's own transmittance. Deeper in the canopy the spectrum becomes richer in far red and poorer in blue and red.

04

Why the forest looks green

What the eye sees is the sunlight that the leaves send back, not the sunlight they take in.

Light that reaches your eye from a leaf is sunlight multiplied by the leaf's reflectance at each wavelength. Chlorophyll takes out the blue and red, so the reflected light is dominated by green. The light that gets through to the floor loses most of its blue and red, so it looks green-gray and carries relatively more far red.

05

Reading the forest from orbit

A satellite sees only a few broad bands of light. A simple ratio between two of them separates vegetation from soil and water.

The Normalized Difference Vegetation Index, or NDVI, compares near-infrared reflectance with red reflectance: (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red). Healthy vegetation reflects strongly in the near infrared and absorbs red, so NDVI is high. Bare soil reflects both, so it sits low. Water absorbs almost everything, so it is negative. 06

What one spectrum cannot tell you

The same index can change for reasons that have nothing to do with the forest itself.

Shadows, soil moisture, the angle of the sun, the mix of tree species, and the size of a satellite pixel all change the measured spectrum. A dense forest and a wide field of healthy grass can produce similar NDVI. Careful work combines several bands, many dates, and ground measurements, and it checks the results against the physics above.

The forest is not a single color. It is a set of filters, each removing a different part of the light, and the light that finally reaches a sensor or a seedling carries a record of every layer it passed through.
