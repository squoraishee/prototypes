The nightly commute

Every night, a vast number of small animals rise hundreds of meters from the twilight zone to feed near the surface, then sink back down before dawn. It is often called the largest daily migration on Earth. Lanternfish and the tiny crustaceans they eat make the trip because predators that hunt by sight can't catch them in the dark.

In this simulation, each dot follows those rules. Lanternfish (blue lights) chase zooplankton (pale specks). Tuna near the surface catch lanternfish, but only when there is enough light to see. When lanternfish sink at dawn, they leave droppings in deep water, which carries carbon down from the surface. A sperm whale dives now and then to hunt squid.

When a whale falls

Most food in the deep arrives as marine snow: a slow drizzle of dead plankton, waste, and mucus. Sometimes a whole meal arrives at once. When a whale dies and sinks to the seafloor, its body feeds a community that changes over the years in three overlapping stages.

Years after the whale lands

Stage lengths vary with the size of the whale and the depth. Researchers have followed carcasses on the seafloor off California since the early 1990s.

A food web that runs on falling scraps

Almost everything in the deep depends on food made in the sunlit surface. Some of it is carried down by migrating animals. Some drifts down as marine snow. Some arrives as a whale. The creatures that live there have adapted to make the most of each delivery, with huge mouths, glowing lures, and bodies built to wait a long time between meals.

Creature drawings are illustrations, not to scale with one another. Depths are typical ranges. The commute and whale fall animations simplify real processes to show how they work.
