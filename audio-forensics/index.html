Audio forensics

Reading a suspicious recording

Faking a voice is cheap now. A few minutes of someone talking is enough to clone them, and the clones are good enough to fool family members, bank call centers and reporters. So when a clip surfaces of a politician saying something outrageous, or a CEO approving a wire transfer, somebody has to work out whether it's real.

That job often falls to an audio forensic examiner. There is no single machine that prints "fake." Examiners look at a recording from several directions: the shape of the sound wave, how its energy spreads across pitches, how steady the voice is, whether a hidden watermark is still there, and what happened to the file on its way to them.

The five graphics below walk through those checks one at a time, using a made-up recording built to show what an examiner looks for.

01

Zoom into the waveform

A waveform plots air pressure over time. From far away you can see words and pauses. Zoom in 150 times and each bump is one opening and closing of the vocal folds, about 7 ms long. In a real person those cycles never repeat exactly, so a voice that is too regular at this scale is a warning sign.

02

Scan the spectrogram

A spectrogram turns sound into a picture. Time runs left to right, pitch runs bottom to top, and brighter means louder. The stacked stripes are the harmonics of a voice. Here, two stretches two seconds apart match down to the background noise. Real speech never repeats that precisely, which points to audio that was copied and pasted.

Move across the spectrogram to scrub.

03

Measure the wobble

Nobody holds a note perfectly. A real voice wavers a little from one cycle to the next, and examiners measure that as jitter and shimmer. The suspect clip follows a believable melody, but up close its pitch line is almost perfectly smooth. Not every measure gives it away. Its breath noise sits right inside the normal range.

04

Squeeze the watermark

Some AI voice tools hide an inaudible signature in the audio they produce. Whether it survives depends on where it is hidden. Compressing a file throws away the highest frequencies first, so a mark tucked up there disappears quickly. A mark spread through the pitches the voice itself uses lasts much longer. Drag the slider to compress the file yourself.

05

Follow the file's history

By the time a clip reaches an examiner, it may have been exported, edited, uploaded, played through a speaker and recorded again on a phone. Each step wipes out some evidence, like the file's original metadata, and adds new traces, like compression artifacts or the echo of a room. The real question is whether those traces fit the story of where the recording came from.
